1 Purpose

Model comparison should follow model diagnosis, not replace it. This vignette develops a structured workflow for convergence, singularity, residual patterns, simulation diagnostics, influence, likelihood-based comparisons, small-sample tests, and parametric-bootstrap comparisons.

2 1. Learning objectives

You will learn to:

  • interpret convergence and singularity diagnostics;
  • inspect residual data before inference;
  • use DHARMa simulation diagnostics for supported GLMMs;
  • conduct grouped influence analysis;
  • compare candidate models using information criteria without automatic selection;
  • perform fixed-effect LRTs under ML;
  • use Kenward-Roger and parametric-bootstrap comparisons for suitable LMMs;
  • understand why a valid comparison depends on the estimand and fitting method.

4 3. Residual plot

if (exists("fit")) {
  mixed_plot(fit, type = "residuals")
}

Look for:

  • nonlinear residual trend;
  • funnel-shaped variance;
  • clusters of extreme residuals;
  • unexplained grouping pattern;
  • serial or spatial dependence that requires a dedicated test or plot.

5 4. Singularity

A singular LMM has one or more random-effect dimensions estimated on or near a boundary. Common signs include a variance near zero or an extreme random-effect correlation.

A singular fit can mean:

  • the data do not support the requested random structure;
  • the design provides too little repeated information;
  • a random slope is unnecessary;
  • predictors are poorly scaled or nearly redundant.

It does not mean that the optimizer necessarily failed.

6 5. Create a deliberately demanding random structure

if (requireNamespace("lme4", quietly = TRUE)) {
  z_sparse <- subset(z, time %in% c(0, 1, 5))
  demanding <- mixed_random_slopes(
    z_sparse,
    response = "height",
    fixed = "treatment * time",
    slope = "time",
    group = "subject",
    correlated = TRUE

  mixed_diagnose(demanding)

9 8. Information criteria as descriptors

Fit two models under ML because their fixed-effect structures differ.

    height ~ time + (1 | subject),
    data = z,

15 14. A model-comparison decision tree

Use the following logic.

Question A: Are fixed effects different?

  • For lmer likelihood-ratio comparison, fit under ML.
  • For small-sample Gaussian LMM inference, consider Kenward-Roger or parametric bootstrap when appropriate.

Question B: Are random or covariance structures different?

  • Keep the scientific fixed-effect estimand comparable.
  • Use likelihood descriptors and diagnostics.
  • Prefer structures supported by the design and repeated-measure geometry.

Question C: Are model families different?

  • Diagnose distributional adequacy.
  • Compare predictive implications and biological process assumptions.
  • Do not rely on a single criterion.

17 16. What not to do

Avoid workflows such as:

  1. generate ten random structures;
  2. rank by AIC;
  3. keep the minimum;
  4. ignore convergence warnings;
  5. report only the final p-values.

This process disconnects modeling from experimental design and understates selection uncertainty.

18 17. Practice exercises

  1. Fit random-intercept and random-slope models with the same fixed effects. Compare their diagnostics before AIC.
  2. Fit Poisson and negative-binomial count models. Ask whether the more flexible distribution solves a residual problem.
  3. Compare AR(1) and heterogeneous AR(1) models. State the additional scientific assumption introduced by heterogeneous residual variances.
  4. Refit a fixed-effect comparison under REML and verify that the guarded LRT refuses the invalid comparison.

19 18. Reporting checklist

For every model comparison, report:

  • models being compared;
  • what differs between them;
  • fitting method;
  • comparison method;
  • diagnostic status;
  • rationale for the preferred representation;
  • whether substantive conclusions are stable.

20 19. Key take-home messages

  • Diagnostics precede model ranking.
  • Convergence and singularity are different concepts.
  • Information criteria are descriptors, not scientific decision rules.
  • Fixed-effect LRTs with lmer require appropriate ML fitting when the fixed structure changes.
  • Influence identifies sensitivity, not automatically erroneous observations.
  • Small-sample methods answer specific inferential questions and do not replace design reasoning.
