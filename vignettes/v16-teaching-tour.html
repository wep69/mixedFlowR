1 Purpose

mixedFlowR was designed not only as an analysis interface but also as a teaching scaffold. The mixed_tour() function exposes conceptual sequences that can be used in lectures, workshops, laboratory exercises, self-study, and graduate methods courses.

This vignette turns those tours into a ten-session instructional pathway.

2 1. Learning objectives

After completing this vignette, an instructor or self-learner should be able to:

  • use mixed_tour() to retrieve teaching sequences;
  • organize topics from design to advanced mixed models;
  • separate conceptual learning from computational complexity;
  • build classroom exercises around frozen teaching datasets;
  • integrate figures, tables, diagnostics, and interpretation;
  • use the same dataset to show progressive model refinement.

12 11. A ten-session course design

Session Core question Main functions
1 What is the experimental unit? mixed_design_audit(), mixed_hierarchy()
2 Why does split-plot have two errors? fit_legacy_splitplot(), splitplot_ems()
3 What is partial pooling? mixed_random_intercepts(), mixed_blup()
4 Why random slopes? mixed_random_slopes(), mixed_covariance_plot()
5 How do non-Gaussian responses change the model? mixed_count(), mixed_binomial(), mixed_beta()
6 Where does repeated-measure correlation enter? mixed_ar1(), mixed_heterogeneity()
7 How should dose be interpreted? mixed_trend(), mixed_curve(), mixed_optimum()
8 How sensitive are conclusions? mixed_robust(), mixed_boot()
9 Why model distributions/spatial/MET structure? mixed_gamlss(), mixed_spatial_field(), mixed_gxe()
10 How do we communicate and reproduce the analysis? mixed_plot(), mixed_table(), mixed_report()

13 12. Three-level teaching pattern

For every major function, use three examples.

Level 1: minimal syntax
Show what the function does.

Level 2: realistic agronomic problem
Connect design, data, and interpretation.

Level 3: problematic or discrepant scenario
Show what can go wrong, such as missing cells, singularity, contamination, overdispersion, boundary values, or spatial confounding.

This pattern is consistent with the public API example catalog.

14 13. Figures as teaching objects

Do not show a figure only as decoration. Ask students to identify:

  • observed versus fitted information;
  • uncertainty;
  • grouping structure;
  • residual pattern;
  • extrapolation;
  • scale and units.

15 14. Tables as teaching objects

A table exercise should ask:

  • What is the estimand?
  • What does the standard error represent?
  • Which random component is associated with which experimental unit?
  • What adjustment was used for multiple comparisons?
  • Is the estimate on the link scale or response scale?

16 15. Suggested practical assessment

Give students one frozen dataset and require:

  1. design audit;
  2. raw-data plot;
  3. justified model specification;
  4. diagnostics;
  5. one alternative/sensitivity analysis;
  6. one publication-ready figure;
  7. one publication-ready table;
  8. a 200-word interpretation;
  9. reproducibility information.

Grade the reasoning, not only whether the software runs.

17 16. Teaching Bayesian and heavy modules responsibly

For computationally expensive modules:

  • run prevalidated examples in class;
  • provide the full code;
  • use eval=FALSE in routine package vignette builds;
  • give students smaller syntax-check configurations;
  • separate computational convenience from final analysis settings.

18 17. Self-study checklist

A learner is ready to advance when they can explain:

  • the experimental unit without looking at the formula;
  • why a random effect was included;
  • why a response family was selected;
  • why a covariance structure is plausible;
  • what a diagnostic failure means;
  • what an EMM, trend, BLUP, and posterior draw represent;
  • what uncertainty measure is shown in a figure.

19 18. Exercises

  1. Use mixed_tour() to write a five-slide lesson outline for random effects.
  2. Create a contamination scenario and ask a peer to decide whether exclusion is justified.
  3. Compare a qualitative treatment analysis with a quantitative dose analysis of the same data.
  4. Build a one-page practical report containing one figure, one table, and four interpretive bullets.

20 19. Key take-home messages

  • Teaching should preserve the connection between experimental design and model structure.
  • The same dataset can illustrate progressive model refinement.
  • Heavy computation should not force all vignettes to eval=FALSE.
  • Interpretation exercises are as important as code exercises.
  • A good mixed-model course teaches what not to model as well as what can be modeled.
